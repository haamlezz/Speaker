Stage 1

What the research says

Music runs from about 20 Hz (wavelength 17 m) to 20 kHz (17 mm). No single driver covers a 1000:1 wavelength range well. Big cones move the air bass needs, but they beam and break up at high frequencies. Small domes spread treble evenly but cannot move enough air for bass. So the design splits the job into three bands and gives each driver a cabinet built for its band.

Bass: Thiele & Small

Thiele (1961) and Small (1972–73) showed that a driver plus its box behaves as a high-pass filter. Its shape is set by a few driver numbers: Fs, Qts and Vas. A vented (bass-reflex) box adds a tuned air mass in a port, which works like a Helmholtz resonator. That extends bass about one octave lower than a sealed box of the same size. Small also gave limits on port air speed, beyond which the port makes noise.

Cabinet shape: Olson

Olson (1951) measured 12 cabinet shapes. Sharp box edges re-radiate sound and cause response ripples of several dB, and a centred driver on a symmetric baffle is the worst case. Rounded edges and an off-centre driver spread those reflections out. Separately, every cabinet loses up to 6 dB below the "baffle step" frequency (≈ 115 / width in metres), where the sound wraps around the box instead of going forward.

Walls, ports and damping

Harwood's work on cabinet panels shows that bracing and damping beat simply using thicker walls. Thick walls alone can store energy and ring longer. Port studies by Salvatti, Devantier and Button, and later by Bezzola, show that flared port ends cut turbulence noise and compression. They also show a best amount of flare, so more flare is not always better. A common noise limit is about 17 m/s air speed for flared ports.

Wave simulator

This is a live 2-D finite-difference solution of the acoustic wave equation, looking down on the cabinet from above. A tweeter on the front baffle plays a steady tone. Blue is compression and amber is rarefaction. Watch for a second, weaker set of rings starting at each front corner. That is edge diffraction, and it adds to or cancels the direct sound depending on where you listen.

Stage 6

Wood, drivers and electronics

Wood

  • Best: 18 mm Baltic birch plywood, void-free with 13 plies. It is stiffer than MDF (the simulation gives 395 vs 262 Hz for the same panel) and holds screws well. It also copes with humidity much better than MDF, which swells if it gets damp.
  • Budget: 18–19 mm MDF. MDF is acoustically fine if you brace it as drawn and add damping. Seal every edge with paint or lacquer in humid climates.
  • Avoid particleboard and solid pine. Particleboard is weak, and solid pine moves with the seasons, which breaks the air seals.
  • Use a hardwood veneer, oil or paint for the finish. Your choice of finish makes no audible difference.

Drivers (per speaker)

  • Woofer 8 in: Dayton Audio RS225-8. Alternatives: SB Acoustics SB23 series, Scan-Speak 22W series.
  • Midrange 4 in: Dayton Audio RS100-8 or an SB Acoustics SB12 midrange. It only has to play 350 Hz – 2.6 kHz.
  • Tweeter 1 in: SB Acoustics SB26STAC-C000-4 or Dayton Audio RST28F-4. Pick one with Fs under 800 Hz so it handles a 2.6 kHz crossover easily.

The woofer numbers I used are nominal datasheet-style values. Before you cut wood, type the real datasheet values into the box simulator above. If they differ, it will give you a new volume and port length.

Electronics

  • Simplest high end: one 3-channel DSP plate amplifier per speaker, such as the Hypex FusionAmp FA253. The crossover, EQ and amplifiers are in one box.
  • Budget: a Dayton Audio DSP-408 (4 in, 8 out) with a 6-channel class-D amp. Or one ADAU1701-based 4-channel DSP amp board per speaker, using 3 of its channels.
  • Measurement: a calibrated USB mic (miniDSP UMIK-1) with the free REW software. A Dayton DATS V3 is optional, for measuring driver parameters and port tuning.
  • Wiring: 1.5 mm² (16 AWG) copper, three terminal pairs (or a Speakon socket), gold or nickel binding posts.

Assembly supplies

  • PVA wood glue (Titebond II or III), clamps, brad nails or screws for alignment.
  • Damping: self-adhesive butyl or bitumen pads, 2–3 mm, on the side and back panels of the woofer chamber.
  • Absorption: 25 mm polyester or wool felt on the woofer chamber walls. Loose polyester fibre or sheep's wool to fill the mid chamber.
  • M5 T-nuts with hex bolts for the drivers (screws in wood strip out), foam gasket tape, and acrylic sealant for the joints.
  • A 76 mm flared port tube, a router with a 25 mm roundover bit, a circle jig, and spikes or feet.
Stage 7

Build order

  1. Cut all panels square. Lay the two baffle layers together and drill a pilot hole at each driver centre (80, 195 and 385 mm from the top, tweeter 35 mm off-centre). Mirror the tweeter offset on the second speaker.
  2. Rout the driver holes with a circle jig, then the flange recesses in the outer layer only. Glue the two layers into one 36 mm baffle and clamp them overnight.
  3. Chamfer the back of the woofer and mid holes at 45° so the inner layer does not choke the airflow behind the cone.
  4. Glue the sides to the baffle, then fit the top, divider, two window braces and bottom at the heights on drawing B. Fit the back last. Run a bead of sealant along every inside joint, especially around the divider, because the two chambers must not leak into each other.
  5. Fit the port tube through the bottom panel and the lower brace window, and glue it in. Round the hole on the underside of the bottom panel (about 18 mm radius).
  6. Apply the damping pads to the side and back walls of the woofer chamber, and line them with 25 mm felt. Keep 76 mm clear around the inner end of the port. Fill the mid chamber loosely with wool or polyester.
  7. Rout the 25 mm roundover on the front vertical edges and the top front edge. Sand and finish the cabinet.
  8. Run the wires: woofer and mid through a sealed hole in the divider, tweeter alongside. Fill every wire hole with hot glue or sealant. Fit the terminals, gasket tape and T-nuts, then mount the drivers.
  9. Put the cabinet on its four spacer blocks and the plinth. The 40 mm gap must stay open on all sides.
Stage 8

Tuning with DSP

ChannelFilters
WooferHigh-pass 20 Hz LR 24 dB/oct (subsonic, protects the woofer) · low-pass 350 Hz LR 24 dB/oct · low shelf +3 to +6 dB at ~425 Hz
MidHigh-pass 350 Hz LR 24 dB/oct · low-pass 2600 Hz LR 24 dB/oct
TweeterHigh-pass 2600 Hz LR 24 dB/oct · gain about −4 to −6 dB to level-match
AllSame polarity · delay the tweeter and mid so they line up in time with the woofer (measure it)
  1. Check the port tuning. Measure the impedance. The dip between the two bass peaks should sit at 27 Hz. If it is higher, the port is too short. If it is lower, trim 5 mm at a time.
  2. Set the levels. Measure each driver alone at 1 m on the tweeter axis with REW. Set the gains so all three bands match.
  3. Set the delays. Use REW's impulse alignment. The tweeter usually needs about 0.1–0.3 ms of delay.
  4. Set the baffle-step shelf. Use +6 dB if the speakers stand far from walls, or +3 to +4 dB if they are 30–60 cm from the back wall.
  5. Correct the room. Measure at your listening seat and only cut peaks below 300 Hz. Leave the treble alone.
  6. Place the speakers. Put them 50–80 cm from the back wall, toed in toward the listener, in an equilateral triangle with your seat.

The 27 Hz figure is measured in free space. In a normal room, the walls usually add bass below about 60 Hz, so it will sound fuller than the model. For flat output down to 20 Hz at concert levels, add a subwoofer later. The DSP already has spare outputs for one.
